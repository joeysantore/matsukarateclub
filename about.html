Who teaches

I’ve been training for close to twenty years. I earned my nidan at NYU’s karate club, where the instruction runs directly through Richard Amos and Naka Tatsuya. For years I competed in sport karate tournaments across the country – winning some, losing more, learning through both. Eventually I got more interested in the parts of the art that competition rules out. That’s what we train. I lead most classes, with several senior members sharing the teaching.

How we train

Our base is Shotokan, but we treat it as a starting point. We train kata for what they actually contain – the throws, locks, and close-range work that competition-era karate left behind – and we test the application against real resistance.

We follow what holds up, wherever it comes from. The people who’ve shaped how we train include Iain Abernethy and Patrick McCarthy on practical bunkai, Scott Langley and the HDKI, Richard Amos and the WTKO, and Rick Hotton on movement. No single organization owns our thinking.

We don’t compete, and we don’t stand on ceremony. Nobody has to call anyone Sensei. Rank is real and earned, but it doesn’t run the room.

Classes

Training is adults only, and classes stay small on purpose. The room works because everyone in it gets real attention – nobody is going through the motions in the back of a crowd.

There’s no standard class. A session might be one technique, studied closely. It might be all kata, or all partner work, or kata broken down into its applications and then drilled on partners and pads. Some days it’s conditioning and hitting each other. What we do depends on what the room needs that day.

You don’t need experience to start. People walk in having never thrown a punch and leave knowing how – that’s the job.

The name

One last thing, since you made it this far.

Matsu is Japanese for pine, and it’s a quiet nod to a few things at once. Shotokan takes its name from “Shoto” – pine waves, the sound of wind moving through the pines, and the pen name of the man who brought karate to mainland Japan. The Ryukyu pine grows along the Okinawan coast, where the art was born. And a coastal pine is a particular kind of tree – bent by every wind that hits it, but too deeply rooted to come up.

That’s the club, more or less. Bent by good ideas from wherever they come, and not going anywhere.
